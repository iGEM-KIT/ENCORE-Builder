Before you start

Everything you need to know to use the builder: what to prepare, what the builder accepts, which combinations are allowed, and what happens to your sequences. Read it once: the builder points you to the same rules where they apply.

Checklist

  • Pick the nanocompartment. Not sure which one fits your use case? Use Choose a shell in the Open NC Database first; its Select for Builder button hands the shell over to the builder as your own sequence. Nanocompartments beyond the list of the builder are only available this way. The targeting peptides of the Open NC Database that belong to this encapsulin are then offered in the cargo module (see The modules).
  • Have your sequences ready: DNA (CDS) or amino acids, with or without start and stop codon (the builder adds them where needed), and without overhangs or restriction sites added by you. The builder adds the overhangs itself.
  • Know your fusion. Decide what sits in the four modules (Type 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5) around the shell and around the cargo; see The modules.
  • Plan a biosafety review. The built-in screening is a helper check, not a replacement for your institution's review.

The five steps

  1. Nanocompartment Site: choose the shell (or your own) and what sits in the four modules around it.
  2. Cargo Site: your gene of interest (or a library cargo) and the modules around it.
  3. Promoter: the promoter that drives both modules.
  4. Modules: the Golden Gate assembly Level 0 → 1 → 2 with sequences, plasmids and downloads.
  5. Overview: what to order as oligos, primers or DNA.

A step unlocks when the previous one is complete. You can go back at any time; your inputs stay.

Rules for your own sequences

Your own sequence goes wherever the builder offers "Use your own sequence …": the cargo, the shell, and any of the four modules (Type 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5) of either site.

RuleWhat the builder does
Required DNA or amino acids Paste a coding sequence (A/T/G/C, T not U) or an amino acid sequence in one-letter code. It is told apart automatically: if at least 90 % of the letters are A/C/G/T, it is read as DNA, otherwise as protein. FASTA header lines (>name), spaces, line breaks and digits are ignored; other letters are dropped with a warning. A protein is back-translated to DNA for S. cerevisiae, 3 bp per amino acid.

The builder puts an ATG in front of the first coding part of each ORF and a stop codon (TAA) behind the last one, in whichever slot it sits. The stop always follows the last coding part of the ORF: if Type 0.5 is an NCS spacer and Type 0.4 holds a linker, the stop comes behind the linker; if only NCS spacers follow the shell or the cargo, the stop comes directly behind it. The part that carries the stop is marked with an asterisk *. The ORF of the nanocompartment module is read from Type 0.1 to 0.5, the ORF of the cargo module from Type 0.5 to 0.1. If the outer modules are plain NCS spacers, the shell or the cargo itself gets the ATG. An ATG you enter yourself is kept and never doubled; a stop codon you enter at the end is removed with a warning. Under the input field a hint with a button (for example Remove start and stop codon) takes those codons out of the field for you. A stop codon in the middle only gives a warning. A linker needs no start codon of its own.

The builder joins parts with a 4-nt overhang plus a 2-nt spacer, which makes 6 nt, so a part with a multiple-of-3 length keeps everything in frame. Amino acid sequences are always in frame.
Required Cargo length The cargo needs at least 30 bp (10 aa). Your own shell and your own module sequences can be any length.

Automatic Restriction sites You do not have to remove them yourself: the codon optimization removes, without changing the protein, every site of the two enzymes of this toolkit, BsaI (GGTCTC / GAGACC) and BbsI (GAAGAC / GTCTTC), and the sites that the iGEM standards forbid inside a part: SapI (GCTCTTC / GAAGAGC) for the iGEM Type IIS standard RFC 1000, and EcoRI (GAATTC), XbaI (TCTAGA), SpeI (ACTAGT) and PstI (CTGCAG) for the BioBrick standard RFC 10, plus NotI (GCGGCCGC), which RFC 10 asks to avoid. See below.
Automatic Biosecurity screening Every sequence you enter yourself is screened with COMMEC before the design is calculated. A hit never blocks you silently: you review it and confirm before continuing. Toolbox parts are not screened. If the screening service is not reachable, the builder tells you and goes on.
Info Primer structure For every primer pair of a Level 0 part the popup shows hairpin, self-dimer and primer-primer dimer (ΔG in kcal/mol, primer3). This is an estimate for your information; it does not block anything.

Codon optimization

  • Your own sequences are codon-optimized for Saccharomyces cerevisiae automatically; DNA or amino acids alike. The protein stays exactly the same; only synonymous codons change.
  • Toolbox sequences (library parts and library cargos) are used as they are by default. Tick Also optimize the toolKIT sequences next to Calculate construct (step 3) to optimize them as well, see below.
  • The result keeps the codon adaptation index (CAI) at about 0.85 to 0.9 and the GC content at about 35 to 40 %. The same input always gives the same output.
  • None of these restriction sites is left: BsaI, BbsI, SapI (RFC 1000), EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI and NotI (RFC 10), on either strand, also not across the seam to the adapter or to the neighbouring part. No run of seven identical bases is left either.
  • Toolbox parts are plasmids that already exist. Some of them contain RFC 10 or SapI sites (for example the shell E-Tm has an XbaI site and the cargo CiVCPO an EcoRI and a SapI site), so a design that uses them as they are is not automatically RFC 10 or RFC 1000 compatible; the compatibility box of the Registry entry shows it.
  • Also optimize the toolKIT sequences. The check box next to Calculate construct (step 3) optimizes the toolKIT sequences that code for protein (shells, cargos, linkers, markers and targeting peptides) with the same method as your own sequences: yeast codon usage and none of the RFC 10 and RFC 1000 sites. NCS spacers are not optimized: they stay as they are, unless one contains one of these cut sites. Then only the site is removed, with as few synonymous codon changes as possible, and such an NCS counts as a changed part (no Registry link, ordered as oligos). The promoter is not coding and stays as it is. The protein of every part stays the same. A part whose sequence changes is no longer the sequence of its plasmid: it gets no iGEM Registry link in the order lists and is ordered as DNA (as an oligo pair if it is shorter than 150 bp), because there is no template for primers. A part that was already fine stays unchanged and keeps its link. Step 4 tells you which parts changed and whether the Level 2 insert still contains one of the sites. The box is off by default.
  • A DNA sequence that is already well adapted (CAI ≥ 0.85), has none of these sites and no run of 7 or more identical bases is kept as you entered it.
  • You see what happened under the input field, in the Level 0 popup of the part, and in the GenBank note. Level 0 primers, oligos and the DNA list all use the optimized sequence.
  • Another host organism? The builder always optimizes for S. cerevisiae; a sequence you optimized for another organism is re-optimized.

The modules around the shell and the cargo

Each site has five parts, Type 0.1 to 0.5. The middle one (Type 0.3) is the shell or the cargo; the four others are modules you configure.

Nanocompartment site

Type 0.1 & 0.2 sit in front of the shell (N-terminal), Type 0.4 & 0.5 behind it (C-terminal). Sense strand.

Cargo site

Type 0.4 & 0.5 sit in front of the cargo (N-terminal), Type 0.1 & 0.2 behind it (C-terminal). The module sits on the antisense strand, so it points away from the promoter.

What you can put into a module

  • NCS (spacer): a neutral spacer; the default filler. Each Type has its own NCS sequence (from the iGEM Registry).
  • NCS 0.12 / NCS 0.45 (combined spacer): one spacer part that fills two neighbouring slots at once (Type 0.1 and 0.2, or Type 0.4 and 0.5). Only one Level 0 plasmid is needed instead of two. NCS 0.12 uses the sequence of Type 0.1, NCS 0.45 that of Type 0.4.
  • Targeting peptide (TP): only in the cargo module; the matching TP for your nanocompartment is chosen automatically. For the human vault (major vault protein, MVP) this is the intein INT. For a shell you imported from the Open NC Database with Select for Builder, the Open NC Database offers its targeting peptides as choices in Type 0.1 and 0.5 of the cargo module. They are optional.
  • Linker, e.g. GGS or GSSS repeats.
  • Surface display marker, e.g. SpyCatcher or SpyTag, displayed on the outside of the cage.
  • Your own sequence. Toolkit parts you pick from the lists are used as they are, unless you tick Also optimize the toolKIT sequences in step 3. To optimize only one part, search it in the iGEM Registry and enter its sequence under Use your own sequence. The builder then optimizes it.

The list shows only parts that are part of the toolkit as a Level 0 plasmid at that exact site and Type. A part that is not offered in a position does not exist there. A part marked (not built) is planned in the toolkit, but its plasmid has not been built yet: you can still choose it, the builder designs with its sequence, and you order it like your own part (oligos or DNA, see Ordering).

Promoter

  • The central promoter drives both modules: the nanocompartment module to the right (sense), the cargo module to the left (antisense). Both transcripts start at the promoter and run outward.
  • GAL1-10 is a bidirectional, galactose-inducible yeast promoter. BDP306, BDP565 and BDP670 are bidirectional promoters as well (754, 356 and 318 bp).
  • The promoter is part of the Level 2 construct only. It has its own Level 1 plasmid and is not ordered in step 5. The GAL1-10 sequence (665 bp) is the one from the toolkit, with a link to its entry in the iGEM Registry.
  • Use your own sequence. You can enter another bidirectional promoter as DNA (A/T/G/C, T not U). Write it in the direction of the nanocompartment module: its 5′ end lies on the side of the cargo module. It needs at least 150 bp (at most 3000 bp) and must not contain a BsaI or BbsI site, because the assembly would cut it; remove such a site with a point mutation. Sites of the iGEM standards (SapI, EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI, NotI) only give a note: a promoter cannot be recoded like a protein sequence. Your promoter is screened for biosecurity like every sequence you enter.
  • Primers. For your own promoter the builder designs PCR primers (step 5, primers): 5′ clamp, BsaI site, one spacer base and the overhangs of the Level 1 promoter module (ACAT and the Level 2 overhang on the left, the Level 2 overhang and TTGT on the right), then the part that anneals to your promoter. BsaI cuts the PCR product to the Level 1 insert, which ligates into the Level 1 backbone. The PCR product is part of the GenBank download.

Reading the results

  • Level 0: individual parts. One plasmid per part, cloned with BbsI into the Level 0 backbone. Click a part to see it with its overhangs, the cloned plasmid and, for your own long sequences, its primers.
  • Level 1: modules (BsaI). The Level 0 parts of a site are joined through their overhangs into the cargo module and the nanocompartment module; the promoter has its own Level 1 plasmid.
  • Level 2: finished construct (BbsI). Cargo module ← promoter → nanocompartment module in the pESC-HIS backbone.
    Which plasmids does the Level 2 insert fit? The insert is released with BbsI and carries the Level 2 overhangs CCAA (left) and AATG (right); it contains no BbsI and no BsaI site. It can be cloned by Golden Gate into any plasmid whose BbsI acceptor site gives exactly these two overhangs, so it is not compatible with every Golden Gate plasmid: other Golden Gate systems use other overhangs. The insert has no terminators, no selection marker and no origin either; the Level 2 destination pESC-HIS supplies them (the ADH1 and CYC1 terminators sit right next to the overhangs, plus the HIS3 marker and the yeast and E. coli origins), so another vector has to offer them as well.
  • Reading direction. All sequences read 5′ on the left to 3′ on the right and are labelled sense or antisense. The cargo module is shown rotated by 180° so that the cargo reads in its own direction, and is labelled "antisense strand"; plasmids stay in backbone orientation.
  • Placeholders. If a part has no real sequence in the parts database yet, the popup says so; the sequence shown (and downloaded) is a stand-in, and no primers can be designed.
  • Downloads. Everything is available as annotated GenBank, per part, per level and for the complete construct. Level 0 records are named Level0.<type>_<part>_<Cargo|NC>, so the site is visible in the name.

Ordering the parts

Step 5 sorts every part by the cheapest way to get it:

ListWhich parts, and what to do
Oligos Every part shorter than 150 bp; your own or a toolbox part. Order the two oligos, anneal them and ligate the product into the Level 0 backbone; the 5′ overhangs already fit, no digest is needed. The length counts the part itself, without the adapter.
Primers Only for your own sequences of at least 150 bp. PCR them with the primers (annealing part plus TTTT clamp, BbsI site, overhangs and frame spacer) and clone the product into the Level 0 backbone with BbsI. Toolbox parts get no primers.
DNA Every part with a real sequence that is not ordered as oligos, as one double-stranded fragment with adapter. Use it instead of PCR.

Every toolbox part that has an entry in the iGEM Registry shows a badge "iGEM Registry ↗" on the right with a link to it. A part without an entry shows no badge, and your own sequences have none. A toolbox part whose sequence was optimized again (box Also optimize the toolKIT sequences) shows no badge either, because its sequence no longer is the one of the Registry entry.

The Registry sequences can differ from the sequences the builder shows. The entries in the iGEM Registry were optimized and entered separately from this software, so the DNA can differ even when the protein is the same. The usual reasons: (1) Different optimization. The builder codon-optimizes your own sequences with its own algorithm (S. cerevisiae codon table, no BsaI or BbsI sites, no long runs of one base). A Registry entry that was optimized with another tool, for example by a DNA supplier, uses different codons. Toolbox parts are not changed by the builder unless you tick Also optimize the toolKIT sequences in step 3; otherwise they are shown as stored in the toolkit. (2) Start and stop codons. The builder stores a part without ATG and without stop codon and adds them where the reading frame begins and ends, which depends on what you place in the neighbouring slots. A Registry entry may already contain an ATG or a stop codon (or none), so its length can differ by 3 or 6 bp and the start and stop can sit at a different position than in your design. (3) Overhangs and frame spacer. The builder adds the overhangs and the 2 nt GC spacer itself; the Registry entry holds only the part, and for a few parts it carries extra bases (the NCS entries carry the two frame spacer bases). (4) Entries are still being completed. A Registry entry can be corrected later. Use the Registry link to find the part, but order and clone from the sequences shown in the builder (the Level 0 window, the order lists and the GenBank files), and compare the translated protein rather than the raw DNA if you want to check a part against the Registry.

Why 150 bp? Many suppliers make oligos only up to about 100 to 150 nt and charge much more beyond that, while double-stranded DNA is not offered below roughly 125 bp. Lists can be copied, and downloaded as CSV or GenBank.

Background: how the builder works

The information windows of the builder (the i buttons) show the short version. Here is the full text behind each of them.

What is a nanocompartment?

Encapsulins are hollow protein cages that self-assemble and encapsulate cargo proteins inside.

Toolkit shell presets provide a matching TP. Shells imported from the Open NC Database offer optional associated TPs at the Cargo Site. Loading is not experimentally established for every shell variant.

Targeting peptides (TP) from the Open NC Database. A TP is attached to the cargo, not to the shell. The reported placement (N-terminal or C-terminal) of a TP does not establish that it works with every shell variant. If you place it at the other terminus than the reported one, targeting and encapsulation there are not experimentally established by the cited evidence, so check the linker in the neighbouring slot (Type 0.2 for Type 0.1, Type 0.4 for Type 0.5), the fusion orientation and the function. The evidence for the pairing of this TP with the shell (for example wild-type shell system, loading supported) is listed in the Open NC Database. Where the Open NC Database has a curated DNA sequence, the builder uses it and may recode it like any own sequence. For a TP with an amino acid sequence only, the builder designs a coding sequence.

The four modules around the shell (Type 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 and 0.5) are freely configurable: keep them as NCS spacers, or place a linker, a display marker such as SpyCatcher / SpyTag, or your own sequence there. A marker is presented on the outside of the cage; handy for attaching binders, tags or a second protein.

Where the modules sit: Type 0.1 and 0.2 form the N-terminus of the shell and are fused in front of the shell. Type 0.4 and 0.5 form the C-terminus of the shell and are fused behind the shell.

Cargo / gene of interest

The cargo is the protein that should end up inside the compartment, e.g. an enzyme or a reporter like GFP.

You provide the coding sequence (CDS). It is directed into the compartment via the targeting peptide.

Where the modules sit: Type 0.4 and 0.5 form the N-terminus of the cargo and are fused in front of the cargo. Type 0.1 and 0.2 form the C-terminus of the cargo and are fused behind the cargo.

Bidirectional promoter

GAL1-10 is a bidirectional yeast promoter: it drives two genes at once in opposite directions. Both transcripts start (5′) at the promoter and run outward.

The nanocompartment module is read off the sense (plus) strand (to the right), the cargo module off the antisense (minus) strand (to the left); that is why they point away from each other. Inducible via galactose.

The other promoters (BDP306, BDP565, BDP670) are bidirectional as well. With Use your own sequence you can enter another bidirectional promoter as DNA: write it in the direction of the nanocompartment module, so that its 5′ end lies on the side of the cargo module. It needs at least 150 bp, must not contain a BsaI or BbsI site, and is screened for biosecurity like every sequence you enter. The builder then designs the PCR primers with the BsaI site and the overhangs of the Level 1 promoter module.

Start and stop codon

The cargo is fused with the linker and targeting peptide into one continuous protein.

You do not have to add a start or stop codon: the builder puts an ATG in front of the first coding part of each ORF and a stop codon behind the last one, in whichever slot it sits: the stop always follows the last coding part of the ORF (for example behind a linker or marker at Type 0.4 or 0.5 of the NC module, or directly behind the shell if only NCS spacers follow it). The part that carries the stop is marked with a *. A stop codon you enter at the end is removed; an ATG you enter is kept (not doubled).

Basis of the checks. Everything is simple sequence logic, no model: the input is read as DNA if at least 90 % of its letters are A/C/G/T, otherwise as protein; the length must be a multiple of 3; every codon in the reading frame is compared with TAA, TAG and TGA to find in-frame stops; GC content = (G+C) ÷ length; amino acids = length ÷ 3. A terminal stop codon is cut off, and an ATG at the start is not doubled.

Codon optimization for S. cerevisiae

Every sequence you enter yourself (cargo, your own shell, your own sequence in a module) is translated and back-translated with the codon usage of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Kazusa table). The protein stays exactly the same; only synonymous codons change. Library parts are toolkit sequences and are never touched.

How codons are chosen. Each codon is drawn at random, weighted by how often yeast uses it (frequency to the power of 3, so frequent codons dominate), and codons below 10 % of their amino acid's usage are never used. That gives a high CAI (codon adaptation index, 1 = only the most frequent codons) of about 0.85 to 0.9 at a natural GC content of about 35 to 40 %; the same range the VectorBuilder optimizer gives for the same sequences. The draw is seeded with your sequence, so the same input always gives the same result.

What is kept out. An exact search then corrects as few codons as needed so that no BsaI site (GGTCTC / GAGACC), no BbsI site (GAAGAC / GTCTTC) and none of the sites the iGEM standards forbid in a part (SapI for RFC 1000; EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI and NotI for RFC 10) remains (also not across the seam to the overhang adapter and the neighbouring part) and no run of 7 identical bases appears. Without that the Golden Gate digest would cut your part.

A DNA sequence that is already well adapted (CAI ≥ 0.85), free of those sites and without a run of 7 or more identical bases is kept as entered.

Amino acid sequences. Instead of DNA you can paste a protein sequence (one-letter code, no stop codon). It is detected automatically (a sequence whose letters are at least 90 % A/C/G/T is read as DNA, anything else as protein) and back-translated with the same codon choice and the same site-free rule.

Basis of the calculation. Codon usage: Kazusa S. cerevisiae table (frequency per 1000 codons). CAI (codon adaptation index) after Sharp & Li (1987): the geometric mean of w = codon frequency ÷ frequency of the most frequent synonymous codon. Met, Trp and stop codons are not counted (they have only one codon). Choice of codons: weighted random draw (frequency³), seeded by a hash of your sequence, so it is reproducible. Site removal: dynamic programming over all codon choices, i.e. the fewest changes that leave no BsaI, BbsI, SapI, EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI or NotI site on either strand (including the seam to the adapter) and no run of 7 or more identical bases; not a greedy fix. GC content = (G+C) ÷ length. The optimizer is our own implementation; it is not a copy of a commercial tool, only tuned to give a similar CAI and GC range.

Level 0 → 1 → 2

The construct is built in three levels. Each level has its own tab below; this is how they work and how they fit together.

Level 0 · individual parts (BbsI). External sequences are cloned into standardized Level 0 parts using BbsI; internal sites are removed and the fixed 4 nt fusion overhangs are added. Level 0 parts are cloned separately, one plasmid each. They are not joined yet: the Type number alone fixes where each part ends up once BsaI assembles them into the Level 1 module.

Level 1 · modules (BsaI). Several Level 0 parts are joined through their shared overhangs by BsaI into one module (cargo module, NC module) and sit between the acceptor overhangs of the Level 1 backbone. grey acceptor overhangs (BsaI) · orange Level 2 overhangs (BbsI, cut later). Sticky ends as they pair: top strand 5′→3′ at the upper left, bottom strand at the lower right (3′→5′); the right end of one construct matches the left end of the next. Click a module to see its sequence and the Level 1 plasmid.

Level 2 · finished construct (BbsI). The modules plus the promoter form the finished construct in the target vector. Both transcripts start (5′) at the central promoter and run outward (3′): the NC module on the sense (plus) strand (right), the cargo module on the antisense (minus) strand (left). The four modules around the shell (Type 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5) can be NCS spacers, linkers, display markers (e.g. SpyCatcher, displayed on the encapsulin exterior) or your own sequences. A * marks the part carrying the stop codon (each ORF's 3′ end); its position follows the C/N fusion and the modules next to the shell.

Which plasmids does this insert fit? It is released with BbsI and carries the overhangs CCAA (left) and AATG (right). It fits any plasmid whose BbsI acceptor site gives exactly these two overhangs, not every Golden Gate plasmid. The insert has no terminators, marker or origin: the Level 2 destination pESC-HIS supplies them, and another vector has to as well.

Basis of the assembly. The builder simulates Golden Gate cloning as sequence assembly, not as a reaction: each part carries its two 4-nt overhangs, the right overhang of one part must be identical to the left overhang of the next (otherwise the design stops with an error), and the parts are joined through them. Internal BsaI (GGTCTC / GAGACC) and BbsI (GAAGAC / GTCTTC) sites are searched on both strands and reported. Between two fused parts the 4-nt overhang plus the 2-nt GC frame spacer add 6 nt, so the reading frame is kept; the overhangs and the GC spacer are chosen so that none of the junction codons is a stop codon, read in either direction. Enzyme kinetics, efficiency or mis-ligation are not simulated.

How the PCR primers are calculated

Annealing part. At both ends of your part a 25-nt stretch is taken (forward = the 5′ end, reverse = the reverse complement of the 3′ end) and shortened base by base, down to 18 nt, until both primers have a melting temperature of at most 72 °C and differ by at most 2 °C. Tm is the nearest-neighbour value from primer3, calculated on the annealing part only.

Checks. Heterodimers, homodimers and hairpins are evaluated by primer3 on the complete primer, because the 5′ extension changes the secondary structure. Anything critical is listed as a warning. The Q5 annealing temperature is the lower Tm of the pair minus 2 to 5 °C (the hotter the primers, the smaller the offset).

5′ extension. Each primer carries, in front of the annealing part, the TTTT clamp, the adapter flank of this Type (BbsI site, the Level 0 and Level 1 overhangs, for Type 0.1/0.5 also the Level 2 overhang) and the 2-nt frame spacer. The PCR product therefore is exactly the Level 0 construct, ready for BbsI cloning into the backbone.

Why the TTTT clamp? Restriction enzymes cut badly when their recognition site sits at the very end of a DNA fragment. The four extra bases give BbsI room to bind and cut; they are cut off again and never end up in the plasmid. A/T-only bases add no extra GC-rich structure to the primer.

Only when it makes sense. Primers are designed for your own sequences of at least 150 bp. Shorter parts are ordered as an oligo pair, toolbox parts are ordered as DNA. If the insert already contains a BbsI or BsaI site, you get a warning; it has to be domesticated first. Without primer3 only a rough Wallace estimate (2 °C per A/T, 4 °C per G/C) is shown.

Basis of the calculation. Tm: primer3-py (calc_tm), nearest-neighbour thermodynamics (SantaLucia 1998) with primer3's defaults (50 mM monovalent ions, 1.5 mM Mg²⁺, 0.6 mM dNTP, 50 nM oligo). Length balancing: start lengths from 25 down to 18 nt are tried; the first one without structure warnings is kept, otherwise the one with the fewest (see above). Structure checks (shown in the table below the primers): calc_hairpin, calc_homodimer and calc_heterodimer of primer3 give the free energy ΔG of the most stable structure on the complete primer. A structure counts as too stable if ΔG is below −3 kcal/mol (hairpin) or −9 kcal/mol (self-dimer, primer-primer dimer) and it melts above 45 °C, because colder structures do not matter during PCR. Q5 annealing temperature: a simple rule of thumb (lower Tm minus 2 to 5 °C), not the NEB online calculator. Oligo pairs (parts under 150 bp) are not calculated at all: the top strand is the left 5′ overhang plus the part, the bottom strand is the reverse complement of the part plus the right overhang. Without primer3 the Wallace rule (2 °C per A/T, 4 °C per G/C) is used and the structure table is left out.

Optimize the toolKIT sequences

Normally the parts of the toolKIT stay as they are. If you tick this box, the sequences of the toolKIT that code for protein (shells, cargos, linkers, markers and targeting peptides) are optimized again for S. cerevisiae, with the same method as your own sequences, and the sites of RFC 10 and RFC 1000 are removed. NCS spacers are not optimized: only if one contains a cut site, that site is removed with as few changes as possible.

A part whose sequence changes is no longer the sequence of its plasmid: it has no Registry link in the order lists and is ordered as DNA (as oligos if it is shorter than 150 bp). Parts that were already fine stay unchanged.

What is changed and what is not. Only parts that code for protein are optimized. The NCS spacers are not optimized and stay as they are, unless one of them contains a forbidden site: then only that site is removed, with as few synonymous codon changes as possible. The promoter is not coding and stays as it is. The protein of every part stays exactly the same, and the codon choice is reproducible: the same part always gives the same sequence. The forbidden sites are the ones of the own sequences: BsaI and BbsI, SapI (RFC 1000) and EcoRI, XbaI, SpeI, PstI and NotI (RFC 10), on both strands and also across the seams to the adapters.

Biosecurity screening (COMMEC)

COMMEC (Common Mechanism, from IBBIS, the International Biosecurity and Biosafety Initiative for Science) is an open-source tool that checks DNA for similarity to known biosecurity concerns. It is the same kind of check DNA synthesis providers run before they ship an order.

What is screened: only sequences you typed or pasted yourself (cargo, your own shell, your own module parts). Toolkit parts are not screened, because their sequences are known and harmless. If the screening is not running, the builder does not calculate a construct.

Against which database: this installation uses COMMEC's curated Biorisk + Low-concern database (about 1.2 GB): known toxins, virulence factors and regulated pathogens, plus a list of harmless related sequences so common genes are not over-flagged.

Not included: the full protein (NR, ~530 GB) and nucleotide (NT, ~580 GB) taxonomy databases. So there is no best-match against arbitrary organisms; only direct hits against the curated list count, and only a real biorisk hit raises a warning here.

A hit never blocks you silently: you review it and confirm before continuing. The screening runs locally in its own container and is a helper check, not a replacement for your institution's biosafety review.

Basis of the check. Your sequence is compared by sequence similarity (profile HMM searches with HMMER and BLAST-type alignments) against the curated COMMEC databases; the result (Pass, Warning, Flag) is taken over unchanged from COMMEC (version 1.0.4, IBBIS). The builder calculates nothing itself here: it only decides, from COMMEC's biorisk field, whether to show a warning.
